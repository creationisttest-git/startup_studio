The Solution

Introducing the AI Squad

Sixteen AI agents working as one product team, led by your vision. They plan the work together, critique it before committing to it, build in parallel, and pass it through every quality gate before it reaches you. You set the direction at four points. The squad owns everything in between.

▮ Amber: your call.   ▮ Green: runs without you. Every handover is the squad’s, not yours.

What it brings to a tech business

Throughput

Five disciplines moving at once, not queueing

While the backend is being built, the interface is being designed, product copy is being written and analytics are being instrumented. Each agent runs in its own context with its own tools, so the work genuinely happens in parallel rather than one model taking turns with itself. Nothing waits for a person to free up.

Assurance

A standard that does not depend on the day you are having

Every agent is accountable for one outcome, and reviews the work against it: the security model, the brand, the words, the reliability of a deploy. Whoever builds something never signs it off. The review happens whether or not you are paying attention.

Compounding

A team that is better on your fifth product than your first

Work is coordinated through the board rather than through conversation, so the record survives the session. When something goes wrong the lesson is written into the agent that should have caught it, and every project gets it at once. Experience accumulates instead of evaporating.

The discipline most teams skip

Before development begins, the squad must answer one question: how will we know whether this worked? Where that cannot be answered, the work does not proceed. Once something is live, its performance is a matter of record rather than opinion, and anything failing to earn its place is retired rather than left to accumulate.

Sixteen roles, and you

Each role is written to advocate for its own responsibility first. Security argues for safety, design argues for the person using it, and the tech lead argues for shipping. Each makes its strongest case with evidence, and does not concede simply to be agreeable.

That's the point. When every side is properly argued, what comes out is a real middle ground rather than whichever opinion happened to be voiced first. Where they genuinely can't agree, it goes up to the product manager, who decides on the spec rather than on who pushed hardest.
